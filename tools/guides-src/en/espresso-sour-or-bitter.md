Sour and bitter are the two most useful words in dial-in, because they point in opposite directions. Sour means the water pulled out too little. Bitter means it pulled out too much, or pulled out the wrong things. The skill is changing **one** variable by the right amount and tasting again.

## Read the cup before you touch the grinder

| What you taste | What it usually means | First change |
|---|---|---|
| Sharp, sour, thin, little sweetness | Under-extracted | Grind finer, or let more liquid into the cup |
| Bitter, dry, ashy, drying on the tongue | Over-extracted | Grind coarser, or stop the shot earlier |
| Sour **and** bitter together | Uneven extraction (channeling) | Fix puck prep before the grind |
| Watery, flat, no body | Ratio too long or dose too low | Shorter yield, or a slightly higher dose |
| Heavy, muddy, overpowering | Ratio too short | Let more into the cup |

Shot time is a clue that supports the taste, not a replacement for it. A sour shot that ran in 18 seconds and a bitter shot that ran in 40 both tell you the same story as the taste. If time and taste disagree, trust your mouth. (More on that in [how long an espresso shot should take](../espresso-shot-time/).)

## Step 1: lock everything except the grind

Before you adjust anything, make sure the things you are *not* changing really are fixed:

- **Dose.** Weigh it to 0.1 g. A 17.5 g dose one day and 18.5 g the next moves the shot as much as a grind step.
- **Distribution and tamp.** Level the bed the same way each time. A tilted tamp or clumps cause channeling, and no grind setting fixes that.
- **Beans.** Dial in per bag. A new roast date or a new coffee is a fresh start.

## Step 2: change the grind, by an amount you can taste

Grind is the strongest lever and the one that does not change the dose or the drink size. Grind finer for sour, coarser for bitter.

How far? Enough to move the shot time by roughly 3 to 5 seconds. On a stepped grinder that is often one or two steps. On a stepless grinder it is a small turn, a few marks on the collar. A change you cannot taste teaches you nothing, and a change that overshoots sends you back the other way. Change, taste, then decide.

Every grinder has a different sensitivity. One step on one grinder can mean a quarter of the shot-time change of one step on another, and the numbers may even count in opposite directions. Write down which way your grinder's numbers go. This is the part people forget, and it is why a simple log pays off.

## Step 3: if the grind is already right, use the yield

Sometimes the grind is where you want it and the shot is still a little sour or a little bitter. The yield (the weight in the cup) is a gentler lever:

- Slightly sour at 1:2? Try 1:2.3. Letting more water through pulls more out.
- Slightly bitter or harsh? Stop earlier, around 1:1.8.

Light roasts usually want longer ratios (1:2.3 to 1:3). Dark roasts usually taste better shorter. Both are tendencies, not rules.

## Step 4: temperature and freshness, last

If grind and yield cannot get you there:

- **Temperature.** Light roasts tend to open up a little hotter (around 93 to 96 °C), dark roasts a little cooler (around 88 to 92 °C). Many home machines don't let you set it, which is fine.
- **Roast age.** Coffee in the first few days after roasting still holds a lot of CO2, which can make shots fast, sour and uneven. Many people let espresso beans rest one to three weeks. Very old beans taste flat and bitter whatever you do.

## What sour and bitter together means

If one shot tastes both sour *and* bitter, the grind is probably not the problem. Water found easy paths through the puck: over-extracting some parts while barely touching others. Grinding finer usually makes it worse. Instead:

1. Break up clumps and stir the grounds evenly (a needle tool, or a fork if you have nothing else).
2. Level the bed and tamp straight.
3. Watch the stream: it should be one even, honey-coloured thread from the center, not spurts or a sideways spray.

Fix that, then go back to Step 2.

## A worked example

You pull 18 g in, 36 g out, in 21 seconds. It is sharp and sour.

| Shot | Change | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Starting recipe, grinder at 14 | 21 s, sour |
| 2 | Finer by 1 step, grinder at 13 | 26 s, sweeter, slight edge |
| 3 | Same grind, yield to 38 g | 28 s, balanced, sweet |

Three shots, one change each, and a recipe you can write down. The next bag of the same coffee starts at shot 3's numbers instead of from zero.

## Keep a log so you stop re-learning

Dial-in is a conversation between your mouth and your grinder. Without notes you repeat the same shots. Log dose, yield, time, grind setting and a one-word taste, and the pattern shows quickly.

## FAQ

### Why is my espresso sour?
Sour espresso is usually under-extracted: the water ran through too fast or too little of it passed through. Grind finer first. If the shot time is already right, let a little more liquid into the cup, for example 1:2.3 instead of 1:2. Very fresh or very light roasts can also taste sour, and a slightly hotter brew helps.

### Why is my espresso bitter?
Bitter, dry or ashy espresso is usually over-extracted. Grind coarser, or stop the shot earlier. Dark roasts are naturally more bitter, so a slightly shorter yield and a cooler brew temperature can soften them. Stale beans also taste bitter and flat.

### Should I change grind or ratio first?
Change the grind first, one small step at a time, because it has the biggest effect. Use the yield (ratio) for fine-tuning once the shot time is in a sensible range.

### Why is my espresso both sour and bitter?
That combination points to channeling, not to the grind. Distribute the grounds evenly, level the bed and tamp straight, then retest before touching the grinder.

### How many steps should I move my grinder?
Enough to change the shot time by about 3 to 5 seconds. That is often one or two steps on a stepped grinder, or a small turn on a stepless one.
